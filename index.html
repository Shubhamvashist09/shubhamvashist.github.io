Fifteen years ago, the US and Canada stood at the same starting line. Both had world-class natural gas reserves. Both had zero LNG export capacity. Today, the US is the world's largest LNG exporter. Canada just delivered its first major cargo.

The common explanation blames geography, regulation, or timing. That framing is incomplete. The gap is structural — the compounding result of capital hesitancy, regulatory friction, and the absence of a repeatable commercial pathway from FID to first cargo. The data tells that story clearly.

The capacity gap — in numbers

The US entered the market in 2016. Canada's first Phase 1 cargo sailed in 2025. That ~10-year lag is not explained by geology — it is the product of execution speed differences across every stage of the value chain.

Execution is reflected in utilization

Nameplate capacity tells only part of the story. Realized output — how consistently facilities run against what was built and licensed — separates operational excellence from paper capacity. US Gulf Coast terminals, benefiting from a decade of integration with the existing pipeline grid and a deep EPC ecosystem, have pushed utilization well above nameplate at multiple facilities. When Canada executes, it executes well too — LNG Canada's ramp has been impressive — but the US has had a decade head start on the learning curve.

Where US LNG actually goes — and why flexibility is the moat

The US model's true advantage is not just volume — it is optionality. Henry Hub-linked tolling structures give buyers destination flexibility. That means US supply can swing toward Europe when transatlantic premiums spike, then rebalance toward Asia as conditions shift. No other exporting region offers this at scale.

The Europe-dominant share reflects the structural pull created by Russian gas displacement post-2022. Asia's share has grown steadily since — a trend that directly shapes where the next wave of supply (including Canada's) needs to position itself.

The Asian market — a window the US is opening, Canada is targeting

Far East LNG demand is the primary growth engine for the next decade. Today, that market is dominated by Australia and Qatar. The US has secured and grown its position. Canada is just entering. The question is not whether Canada has a place in this market — it clearly does — but whether it enters while long-term contracts are still being signed.

When Canada executes — it executes fast

Here is the nuance most commentary misses: Canada's execution problem was getting to FID, not operating after it. LNG Canada's Phase 1 ramp — tracked against Sabine Pass's first-year ramp using EIA historical data — shows that Canada's first major terminal is ramping significantly faster than the US benchmark did in 2016. The bottleneck was never operational capability. It was the decade-long road to first cargo.

The shipping equation — Canada's geographic advantage, correctly framed

Canada's West Coast offers a structurally shorter route to Asian markets than US Gulf Coast via Panama. Critically: the Strait of Hormuz is a vulnerability for Middle Eastern LNG suppliers — not for US or Canadian cargoes. Canada bypasses both Panama Canal congestion risk and Hormuz supply-chain exposure entirely. That is a genuinely differentiated position in the current geopolitical environment.

The window — real, but not permanent

Canada's second LNG window is the most credible in a generation. The resource is there. The geopolitical demand is there. Japan, Germany, India, and South Korea are actively seeking non-Hormuz, non-Russian supply alternatives. Canada's West Coast ticks every strategic box.

"Canada isn't competing with the US of today. It's competing with the US of 2016 — for the long-term offtake contracts that Asia's buyers are signing right now."

But long-term LNG contracts are being signed now. The buyers filling their 2030s portfolios are making decisions in 2025 and 2026. Every year of delay is not deferred — it is potentially displaced by a competitor who moved faster.

LNG Canada's ramp proves Canada can operate at world-class levels once it gets there. The challenge — regulatory alignment, capital confidence, First Nations partnership — is getting the next projects to FID before the window closes. That is an execution problem. It always has been.
